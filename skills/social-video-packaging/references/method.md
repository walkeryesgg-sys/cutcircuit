## 1. Audience question

Start from tension, not topic.

Weak: “This video is about a new chip factory.”

Strong: “If the best foundries already exist, why would someone risk building another one?”

A useful question satisfies all three:

1. A target viewer cares or quickly understands why it matters.
2. The answer is not obvious from common knowledge.
3. The video delivers an answer supported by evidence.

Question strength, from narrow to broad:

- event: What happened?
- cause: Why is this necessary?
- contradiction: If X is true, why do Y?
- consequence: What changes for the viewer or industry?
- mechanism: How could this work?
- boundary: Is the spectacular claim actually true?

Prefer a concrete contradiction at entry and a larger implication at the end.

## 2. Evidence ledger

Record each title-worthy claim with:

| Claim | Status | Best source | Exact wording allowed | Wording prohibited |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Example: planned area | confirmed plan | official filing | “planned at…” | “has become…” |
| Example: largest building | attributed claim | named speaker | “X says it will be…” | “confirmed world record” |
| Example: single structure | unresolved | render only | “possibly / unclear” | “world's largest single building” |

Use primary sources first: official filings, company pages, transcripts, regulator records, direct posts, and first-party analytics. Use strong reporting to contextualize. Treat viral posts as examples of packaging, not evidence of facts.

## 3. Hook ranking

Score each possible hook from 1–5 on:

- relevance to the intended viewer;
- surprise or information gap;
- immediate comprehensibility;
- visual proof available in the video;
- evidence strength;
- distinctiveness from existing coverage;
- payoff within the opening and full video.

Choose one lead hook. A large number is useful only when viewers can interpret it. Translate abstract scale into a defensible comparison, and name the comparison dimension: floor area, footprint, volume, investment, output, or energy.

## 4. Title structures

Generate across structures before polishing language.

### Direct question

`Why must [recognizable subject] do [costly/unexpected action]?`

Use when the audience owns the question and the video has a clear answer.

### Statement that creates a question

`[Subject] still does X, yet has begun Y.`

Often feels more confident than a question mark while preserving curiosity.

### Scale plus implication

`[Intuitive scale]. What is it actually for?`

Name the measurement. Avoid comparing incomparable numbers.

### Apparent contradiction

`It looks like X, but its larger purpose is Y.`

Ensure Y is supported, not merely poetic.

### Hidden constraint

`The real bottleneck is no longer X—it is Y.`

Best for expert positioning. Explain Y early.

### Consequence

`If this succeeds, [specific system/industry] changes first.`

Avoid pretending a speculative consequence is inevitable.

### Search-led versus browse-led

- Search-led: state the recognizable entity, event, and useful answer in natural query language.
- Browse-led: lead with a concrete tension or consequence that is understandable without prior intent.
- Hybrid: retain the entity while opening a real information gap. Reject hybrids that become long, vague, or keyword-stuffed.

Front-load the words that identify the subject and stake. Put series names, episode numbers, and branding later unless the audience explicitly searches for them. Questions are not inherently stronger than statements; use a question only when it belongs to the viewer and receives an early answer.

## 5. Scoring and rejection

Score surviving candidates out of 100:

| Dimension | Weight |
|---|---:|
| Factual precision and attribution | 20 |
| Audience relevance | 15 |
| Clear topic/entity recognition | 15 |
| Curiosity or tension | 15 |
| Promise-to-content alignment | 15 |
| Platform and account fit | 10 |
| Cover/opening complementarity | 5 |
| Natural, distinctive language | 5 |

Reject before scoring if the title:

- makes an unsupported factual upgrade;
- promises an answer absent from the content;
- confuses campus, building, footprint, floor area, volume, power, compute, production, or capacity;
- falsely frames cooperation as rivalry;
- relies on an unfamiliar term without a familiar entry point;
- uses a question whose obvious answer is “maybe” or “nobody knows.”

Scores guide judgment; they do not replace it. Explain why the primary candidate wins.

## 6. Description construction

The description has four jobs:

1. **Context:** identify the event and why it matters.
2. **Value:** state what the viewer will understand, not every fact covered.
3. **Credibility:** attribute plans, estimates, and contested claims; include primary links when appropriate.
4. **Action:** invite a specific, meaningful response or next step.

Use this flexible sequence:

- line 1: tension or result;
- line 2: promised explanation;
- body: 2–5 concrete points or a short narrative;
- boundary: what is confirmed versus planned;
- sources/chapters when the platform and format reward them;
- one focused CTA;
- a small set of relevant hashtags.

The first one or two lines must work when truncated: identify the object and give a reason to continue. Include important entities and search language naturally, not as a keyword list. For research-heavy work, separate concise audience-facing copy from a source/attribution block. A description cannot repair a misleading title or an opening that delays the promised value.

Do not reveal every payoff in the first line, but do not conceal the value. Avoid “本期视频带你了解…” when a concrete statement works.

## 7. Promise audit

Write the packaging contract in one sentence:

`A viewer who chooses this expects to learn/see ______.`

Then check:

- cover establishes the visual object or emotion;
- title adds the question or significance;
- opening recognizes the promise in the first beat;
- an initial answer arrives early;
- the body earns nuance and scale;
- the ending resolves or clearly bounds the question.

If the opening begins with unrelated biography, channel branding, greetings, or long background, revise it.

Use the three-surface division test:

| Surface | Primary job | Failure pattern |
|---|---|---|
| Cover | Recognition, emotion, visual proof, scale | miniature poster or abstract spectacle |
| Title | Tension, question, consequence, useful promise | repeats cover copy or hides the subject |
| Opening | Immediate acknowledgement and first payoff | restarts from background or changes topic |

A strong package creates one coherent expectation through three complementary signals.

## 8. Analytics diagnosis

Interpret metrics as a funnel, segmented by traffic source and audience when possible.

| Symptom | Likely issue | First test |
|---|---|---|
| Low impressions | topic/audience eligibility or weak history | topic framing, policy, audience fit |
| Impressions but low chosen-view/CTR | packaging clarity or appeal | title-cover thesis |
| High CTR, sharp opening drop | broken promise or slow payoff | first seconds and claim order |
| Good retention, low interaction/share | useful but not identity/socially valuable | stronger consequence or discussion prompt |
| Search traffic weak | entity/query mismatch | searchable title and description language |
| Good initial data, weak satisfaction | exaggerated promise or thin substance | narrow title to actual value |

Do not declare a title winner on CTR alone. Prefer qualified watch time, retention, satisfaction, or the platform's native test winner. Change one major variable per test where practical.

## 9. Testing strategy

Create up to three variants with different theses, for example:

- scale: “How large is it?”
- cause: “Why build it personally?”
- consequence: “What bottleneck does it remove?”

Do not waste a test on punctuation, one synonym, or text color alone. Record the audience and surface, exact title-cover pair, hypothesis, primary outcome such as watch-time share, and guardrails such as opening retention or misleading-feedback rate.

On YouTube, native title/thumbnail tests select by watch-time share rather than CTR alone. Use older suitable videos for lower-risk learning when practical, and allow the platform test enough time to resolve. On platforms without a controlled native test, compare matched publication windows cautiously; do not claim causation from one post.
